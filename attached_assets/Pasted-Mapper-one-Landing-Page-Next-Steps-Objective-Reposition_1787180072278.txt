Mapper.one Landing Page — Next Steps

Objective

Reposition mapper.one from an open-source/recreational mapping project into a polished field mapping platform for people who work outdoors, while preserving enough of its outdoor/adventure identity that it doesn't feel like generic enterprise software.

The central product story should shift from:

An open-source mapping application

to:

Map the places where you work.

Mapper.one lets people record routes in the field, mark important locations, attach photos and notes, and share that knowledge with coworkers, crews, clients, or collaborators.

The strongest initial audiences include:

- Arborists and tree-service companies
- Trail builders and trail-maintenance organizations
- Landscape architects and landscaping companies
- Forestry and land-management professionals
- Parks and recreation departments
- Conservation organizations
- Property and estate managers
- Surveying and field crews
- Construction/site crews
- Farms, vineyards and large-property operators
- Outdoor guides and recreation organizations

The site should communicate that mapper.one was designed for people who actually spend their days outside and on the ground, rather than sitting behind GIS software.

---

1. Move Away From Open Source Positioning

Remove open source as a primary part of the mapper.one identity.

The landing page should no longer lead with GitHub, source code, self-hosting, developer language, or the idea that mapper.one is primarily a community software project.

The commercial product should instead be presented as proprietary mapper.one software and services.

GitHub references should be removed from:

- Primary navigation
- Hero section
- Primary CTAs
- Feature descriptions
- Footer promotional areas

Existing open-source repositories do not necessarily need to disappear immediately, but they should no longer define the brand.

The new brand hierarchy should be:

mapper.one

Field mapping for people who work outside.

---

2. Preserve the Recreational DNA

Do not turn mapper.one into something that visually resembles enterprise asset-management software.

The outdoor roots are actually a competitive advantage.

The experience should still feel appropriate for someone mapping:

- a mountain-bike trail
- a forest
- a 100-acre property
- a landscaping project
- a tree-removal site
- a conservation area
- a backcountry route

Use large outdoor photography, satellite/topographic mapping imagery, GPS tracks, pins, field photos and people working outdoors.

Think:

Outdoor adventure aesthetic + professional field tool.

Not:

Corporate SaaS dashboard + stock-photo construction workers.

---

---

4. Immediately Demonstrate the Core Workflow

Directly underneath the hero, explain mapper.one in three or four visual steps.

Walk it. Mark it. Share it.

1 — Record

Walk, drive or ride through the property while mapper.one records your route.

2 — Mark

Drop GPS waypoints anywhere something matters.

3 — Document

Attach photographs, descriptions and field notes directly to locations.

4 — Share

Give someone else the map so they can find the route, location and information without you being there.

This should become the central product narrative.

---

5. Introduce "Knowledge Attached to Place"

The landing page should emphasize that mapper.one captures field knowledge, not simply coordinates.

Suggested section:

Don't just drop a pin. Explain what's there.

Every waypoint can contain the context someone needs when they reach it.

Show an actual UI example:

White Oak — Remove

📍 35.XXXX, -82.XXXX

[tree photograph]

Notes

Dead upper canopy. Remove entire tree. Access from service road. Avoid septic field east of driveway.

This demonstrates the value proposition much better than describing "custom waypoints."

12. Establish a Visual Identity Around "Outdoor Work"

14. Product Strategy Behind the Redesign

The website redesign should also reflect a larger change in how mapper.one thinks about its product.

Today the fundamental object may effectively be a map/track.

Commercially, the hierarchy should eventually become:

Organization

→ Project / Property / Job

→ Routes

→ Waypoints

→ Photos + Notes

→ People

→ Assignments / Status

This allows the exact same underlying mapper.one technology to serve very different industries without building separate applications.

A tree company calls it a Job.

A landscaper calls it a Property.

A trail organization calls it a Project.

A forester calls it a Tract.

Underneath, they're using essentially the same mapper.one data model.

---

15. Immediate Development Priorities

The landing-page work can happen before rebuilding the entire application.

Phase 1 — Reposition

Remove open-source messaging, rewrite the homepage around field work, improve screenshots/product demonstrations, retain App Store acquisition and add commercial lead capture.

Phase 2 — Industry Pages

Build dedicated landing pages for Arborists, Trail Builders, Landscaping, Landscape Architecture, Forestry and Land Management.

Phase 3 — Commercial Validation

Recruit roughly 5–10 businesses in the strongest categories and watch how they actually use tracks, waypoints, photographs and sharing.

Phase 4 — Teams MVP

Use those businesses to determine which commercial functionality deserves development first rather than attempting to build a complete GIS platform.

Phase 5 — Paid Product

Introduce individual Pro and Mapper.one Teams subscriptions once the commercial workflow has been validated.

---

Core Brand Direction

The most important change is not making mapper.one look more "business-like."

It's changing the reason someone thinks it exists.

Old perception:

«Cool mapping app for creating and sharing routes.»

New perception:

«A simple way to capture everything you know about a place and share it with the people who work there.»

That leaves plenty of room for hiking, biking and exploring while creating a much stronger reason for an arborist, trail organization, landscaper, forester or land manager to actually pay for mapper.one.
